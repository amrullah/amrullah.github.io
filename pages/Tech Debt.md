- #[[Career Learnings]]
- ## So It's Time to "Do Something" About Tech Debt
  
  First off: good for you. The first step to recovery is admitting you have a problem.
  
  Now the hard part: Tech Debt (TD) management is not only inherently difficult, it is almost pathologically impervious to the standard playbook of the tech industry.
  
  Most organizations, when they get to the point where they decide to "do something" about TD, will kick off a committee, tiger team, or working group. A good mix of senior leaders and passionate engineers will show up, and the first item on the agenda will inevitably be "How do we define tech debt?" We need to put boundaries on the problem, after all, and figure out what is in-scope.
  
  Anecdotally, most efforts die off at this phase after weeks bogged down on the definition.
  
  The lucky ones will then progress to "How do we measure tech debt?" In many cases, the groups with the worst definitions will come through the "measurement" phase quickly: "Tech Debt is just how many static analysis errors and compiler warnings we have, so count those." Groups with more robust definitions tend to fail at the measurement phase.
  
  The lucky few will find a reasonable definition and a few reasonable proxies for measurement and start proposing concrete work to be done. Unfortunately, this has taken so long that management buy-in has already started to wane, but they get permission to prioritize a few tasks. But when planning for next year starts, TD doesn't have a seat at the table, and to get further support, they need to prove the impact of the work so far.
  
  A few months later, the sync meeting for that working group gets canceled. Rigorously proving impact was too hard. Nobody has shown up for several meetings in a row, and we just feel a vague sense of awkward shame about the whole thing.
  
  This doc is an attempt to skip past all the completely standard hurdles and get your org to the point where you can start to gain traction and make things better, quickly. But it is by no means a complete solution: plans and ideas are presented in the abstract here, because you need to think about them and make them your own. If you follow this document *rigorously*, you will miss the forest for the trees. If you read through this and see how to apply these ideas to your own context and make this framework your own, you have far better chances.
  
  This also cannot be a silver bullet: there is no easy answer to cleaning up our long-neglected TD backlogs. It is going to be challenging work, and it is going to take years in most cases. This is a matter of education, prioritization, and culture change for your team, your org, and your product. It will take some time, and a lot of energy. But it can be done.
- ## Tech Debt 101
  
  To get started, we need *some* working definition for TD. There are lots to choose from:
- Ward Cunningham originally [coined the term "technical debt"](https://youtu.be/pqeJFYwnkjE?si=CfhN0-rSxTx-KhcW&t=26): "If we fail to make our program align with what we then understood to be the proper way to think about our ... objects then we were going to continually stumble over that disagreement, and that would slow us down, which is like paying interest on a loan."\ \ Ward's original formulation is interesting because he specifically used a financial metaphor because everyone has some understanding of finance, and the project at the time was financial software. The metaphor was chosen for its power to be persuasive, not necessarily rigorous. And the original metaphor was purely about refactoring to fix design issues - fundamentally, Ward was talking about the same thing as Fred Brooks in "The Pilot System" chapter of The Mythical Man Month. The first working implementation of a system teaches us the true nature of the problem, and only after rework or a full rewrite do we have an implementation that properly suits that problem.\ \ One critical component of Ward's formulation: unaddressed TD **slows us down**.
- Academics and industry researchers settled on a [definition](https://youtu.be/uwWicvn8Yz0?si=jIhnsZmR0_hzowoa&t=43) around 2017, "Design or implementation constructs that are expedient in the short term, but set up a technical context that may make future changes costly."\ \ This captures one critical point that Ward's definition didn't mention explicitly: it isn't that TD is going to slow down unrelated new development, it's slowing down changes to the affected system, and to a lesser extent anything using a debt-riddled interface.\ \ One point that this definition gets wrong, however, is by implying that TD arises because of shortcuts and technical expediency. TD *may* arise because we rush things, but it may also happen purely because the world changes around us. Using Java 8 in 2015 is perfectly sensible, that's the current version. Using Java 8 in 2024 is TD that deserves to be addressed. Deciding to upgrade in 2015 isn't "expedient." The only "shortcut" in this story is choosing to not upgrade in the meantime.\ \ The more fundamental point is that TD arises because of *change*. Maybe we learned something, as in Walt's original formulation. Maybe the resources available to work with changed and now we can go back and build better designs, as in the academic formulation. Maybe new software or hardware has changed the appropriateness of our existing solutions. But at root: **TD arises because something changed**.
- Other definitions abound. There are literally hundreds of essays on Medium, and often they include a definition like "the accumulation of sub-optimal technical decisions made overtime [sic]."\ \ Even poorly written definitions like this have something to add to the discussion: this short definition doesn't even explicitly say that TD manifests only in our code and design! After all, if we have a completely manual testing or release process, does that not feel like technical debt as well? **Deciding something is or is not TD is less important than addressing engineering inefficiencies.**
  
  The critical point here is that most definitions are good enough, and the more precise we try to make it, the less satisfying the result. If your team doesn't already have strong feelings about a definition, read on.
- ### Forms of Tech Debt
  
  To reinforce the point that tech debt can mean many things, Engineering Productivity researchers at Google have [studied what engineers mean by technical debt](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/10109339). Initially based on direct interviews and then validated by surveying thousands of engineers, their taxonomy covers a wide range of factors. Specifically, the Google EPR tech debt taxonomy lists:
- Migration is needed (or in progress)
- Documentation on project and APIs
- Testing
- Code quality
- Dead and/or abandoned code
- Code degradation
- Team lacks necessary expertise
- Dependencies
- Migration was poorly executed or abandoned
- Release process
  
  Clearly, the term "tech debt" has grown beyond Ward's original formulation: it isn't just design and code quality.
  
  Notably, several of these topics are addressed specifically in research on engineering productivity through venues like DORA and the State of DevOps reports. Testing (including continuous integration), documentation, and release processes are major factors in the DORA research on technical effectiveness.
- ### A Working Definition, and Urgency
  
  If you need a specific suggestion, try this: **Tech debt is the collection of low-urgency productivity issues in our project, the difference between the system we have and the system we wish we had.**
  
  Critically, this adds the point of "low urgency," which is missing in the earlier discussions. **If a software issue was urgent, we'd call it a bug and we'd go fix it.** We wouldn't be having meetings about it, and you wouldn't be reading guidance on how to track and prioritize it.
  
  In terms of the [Eisenhower Matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_management#The_Eisenhower_Method), TD issues have various levels of **importance** based on how much they slow us down, and how much our messy systems are still under ongoing development and maintenance. But unlike a missing feature or a user-critical bug, TD issues usually have very low **urgency**. They will be just as important (or not) tomorrow, next week, or next quarter.
  
  The tech industry is often laughably bad when it comes to handling important-not-urgent issues, further contributing to the challenge of properly handling TD.
- ### Measuring Tech Debt
  
  Imagine we had access to a true [Oracle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oracle) and could ask, "Oh great Oracle, please tell me how much tech debt is in my project?" To respond with a quantitative measure, the Oracle clearly must have some unit system to use for the measurement. What are the units of TD? That is, do we measure it by weight, mass, velocity, electric charge? Certainly not.
  
  When pressed, people tend to give one of three answers:
- **Cost** - This comes in two flavors. The easier to estimate is "How much would it cost to fix?" Estimates for cleanup work are substantially less accurate than estimates for new development, but in theory we could describe how much engineering time and compute resources would be needed to fix the mess. But that doesn't really motivate cleaning it up, especially to a skeptical PM or Engineering Director. What we need to know is the return on investment (ROI) of cleaning it up: not only how much it will cost, but how much we would save over the next few years if we did so. Even that is less than convincing, however, because it assumes that all of our TD is worth fixing, which as we will see is dramatically not the case.
- **Time** - Largely this is the same as Cost, but ignoring effects other than the engineering time involved. We either present "hours to clean everything" or the ratio of that to hours saved.
- **Friction** - Some of our TD definitions described that TD "makes future changes more costly." That usually isn't a constant factor (e.g., "All changes to this system will take 1 extra day"), but could reasonably be assumed to be roughly linear ("All changes to this system take 10% more work than we think they could").\ \ Looking at it this way: **Tech Debt is the important-not-urgent difference between the system we have and the system we wish we had, and it manifests as a tax or friction on productivity.**
  
  If we accept that reasoning, we're in great trouble. The tech industry loves statements like "You cannot improve anything without measuring it." (This clearly risks causing issues with [Goodhart's Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goodhart%27s_law) and the [McNamara Fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McNamara_fallacy).) However, [**engineering productivity cannot be measured directly**](https://queue.acm.org/detail.cfm?id=3454124), and even if it could, we certainly cannot reliably measure the delta in productivity between the system that we have and a counterfactual world where we've fixed the productivity issues.
  
  Any TD program required to provide **accurate** measurements is doomed to failure. Focus on directionally accurate measures, find good proxies, and listen to the stories your engineers are telling.
- ### What Can We Measure? 
  
  In some (rare) cases, major classes of tech debt for a particular project may be well understood and easily tracked. Counting the number of clients that need to be migrated, or tickets that need to be closed, is certainly a measurement of remaining work. It's not measuring the impact or cost of TD, but it does track what needs to be done. Sometimes, with sufficient support from management, that suffices.
  
  In most cases, when asked to measure either the extent or impact of TD, we are going to have to rely on indirect proxies. One of the easiest and most robust proxies for "true" TD measurement is quite simple: **ask the engineers how bad it is.** Developer surveys have become common in many companies over the last few years, and one type of question is common for exactly this reason:
- "How much time are you losing because of technical debt?"
- "How affected are you by technical debt?"
- "What fraction of your time is wasted because of inefficient systems, bad designs, etc?"
  
  These questions are trying to get at the core issue: what is the coefficient of friction for our project, and how is that changing over time?
- ### Is "Debt" Even the Right Metaphor? 
  
  The upside to the "debt metaphor," as Ward Cunningham termed it, is that everyone has at least a general sense of finance. The notion of a tax rate or interest rate is intuitive, and does at least suggest that we are better off acting on these issues sooner.
  
  On the other hand, there are no financial instruments in the world where we cannot *measure* the interest rate. Reasonable people have wildly different estimates of how much we are "paying" for any given bit of TD.
  
  Maybe more tellingly: in finance, one unit of goodness (asset) can fully cancel one unit of badness (debt). One unit of very well designed, easy to use, productivity boosting code does not ever "cancel" a bit of TD - at best the boost provided might negate the drag caused by some piece of TD, but both are still present and may change over time. Also, **it is far cheaper to prevent a tech debt issue than to clean it up after the fact.** This suggests finance may not be the right guiding metaphor.
  
  There is some support instead for "pollution", and there is a lot to appreciate with "technical pollution" as the guiding metaphor:
- Prevention is cheaper than remediation
- There are countless different pollutants, which require different approaches to tracking and resolving
- Pollution has a lot of "externalities," in that the people that cause the mess aren't necessarily the ones saddled with the results
- Some messes are small and expected -- a messy kitchen after a big meal is natural, and the cleanup should be considered part of the task. This dovetails nicely with ideas of "engineering excellence."
- Some messes are big and daunting. In the US, [Superfund Sites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Superfund_sites) are places deemed so polluted as to require extensive long-term expert handling to mitigate the issue and eventually rehabilitate the land. Some redesigns and deferred upgrades feel like this.
- Some things may originally be considered great and only later become recognized as pollutants. Asbestos was an incredible improvement on fire proofing our buildings, until we understood the health risks. Some design patterns (e.g., Singletons) go through similar cycles of popularity followed by distaste, fear, and disapproval.
  
  "Pollution" is not a perfect term, however. The connotation on it is far too strong: many of us aren't willing to say, "I'll pollute in the short term to get this release out, and try to clean it up later." In business, the option to cut a few corners in the short term to meet a deadline may be necessary and wise, so long as we handle the aftermath responsibly. The connotation on "technical pollution" is so negative that it may run counter to this need.
  
  In practice, the term "Tech Debt" is here to stay. It is unfortunate that some of the financial metaphor baggage gives misleading notions of how to manage it. However, so long as we understand that it's about important-not-urgent productivity hindrances, we can hopefully avoid over-anchoring on any metaphor. We should be willing to borrow from finance, pollution, or any other guiding framework that we find useful, so long as it helps us align our teams and stakeholders. The goal is improving productivity and avoiding deferred maintenance crises.
- ### Tech Debt 101 Guidance
- Tech debt is the friction (or tax) on productivity, arising from important-but-not-urgent issues in our project, as measured against the ideal state of the project as we understand it today.
- We cannot measure either the scope or cost of tech debt directly. We cannot measure productivity directly, and we certainly cannot measure the loss in productivity between the world we live in and the counterfactual world we wish we had.
- Any definition of TD that focuses too much on "shortcuts" or "expediency" will ignore issues of deferred maintenance, upgrades, etc.
- Any definition of TD that ignores urgency will probably be easy to confuse with bugs, outages, and other easy-to-prioritize issues.
- It is up to you and your team whether you want to limit TD to design, code, etc. It is perfectly acceptable to ignore those questions and focus instead on the impact: TD hurts engineering productivity. If a slightly broken keyboard is hurting your productivity, arguing about whether it is TD or not isn't the important thing. Fixing the issue is what matters.
- Do not worry too much about precise definitions.
- Accept proxy measurements, because precise ones can never exist. Asking the people that are most hurt by TD how much it hurts seems like a rational choice.
  
  With these ideas in hand, we can move past the normal "getting started" hurdles and start with the hard work: doing something about it.
- ## TD Management Requirements
  
  A successful strategy for Tech Debt management is going to require several important ingredients:
- An inventory of outstanding TD issues
- Periodic prioritization and estimation activities for that inventory
- Some allocation of time for engineers to tackle TD (or other important-not-urgent issues)
- An escalation path for TD issues that are beyond the scope of the team/product/organization
  
  Let's discuss basic guidance for all of these.
- ### Keep an Inventory
  
  If we abstract enough to understand that TD is really the phenomenon of "our engineering team ought to be more productive than this," usually (but not always) stemming from issues in existing code and technical systems, it's clear that we can't build a tool that comprehensively identifies and measures all of our TD. But we don't need an objective and automated tool, we need to listen to our developers: What parts of our team, codebase, policies, etc, are frustrating you and slowing you down the most?
  
  Each team needs a **tech debt inventory**. This needs to be easy to contribute to, stable over time, and visible to the team. It's moderately likely that the lowest-priority issues in your issue tracker (Jira) are already a reasonable seed for that inventory.
  
  The storage for your inventory can be a spreadsheet (powered by a web survey form), or distinct view / component in your issue tracker. Generally, it's best to use tools that are already in use by the team, and to keep the inventory management as simple as possible. You don't want to write new software for this or spin up a complex database - very few teams are going to identify more than a couple hundred issues.
  
  An unusual approach to crowdsourcing an inventory can also give us at least a rough prioritization. Use of audience question crowdsourcing tools like [Dory](http://dory.app/) or [Slido](http://slido.com/) can allow for the team to use the same UI for suggesting TD inventory items and perform some basic prioritization on that inventory.
  
  Remember, presence in the team inventory does not necessarily mean a commitment to **do** anything about those TD items. We know that TD issues individually are not urgent, but not everything in TD is important enough (or easy enough) to warrant committing time to. It is nice to imagine that over time the TD inventory should dwindle, but in reality we want to primarily put bounds on the most substantial TD issues (driving those down or at least holding them constant over time).
- ### Prioritize/Estimate ROI 
  
  Given a list of our TD items, where do we start? Before we dive in, we need a mechanism to roughly sort our problems in order to begin working on the highest value items in the inventory. There are a few common approaches to this prioritization, but only one we recommend.
- Quickest first - Get time-to-fix estimates from the team, handle as many items as possible, starting with the easiest first. The primary issue here is that natural human behavior has probably already let us handle the high-value/low-cost items. In practice, we can spend a good deal of time fixing small TD issues without materially affecting productivity.
- Longest first - The inverse of the above, starting with the hardest problems first. The issue here is that the difficulty in fixing an item is only loosely correlated with the value in doing so. Some of the hardest issues to mitigate may well be low-impact.\ \ Just as critically, TD cleanup is a meaningfully different skill than new development. Starting a TD program with the hardest problems instead of finding high value warm-ups is a common way to cause the team to over-estimate the true cost of TD improvement.
- Highest-impact first - Get estimates from the team, not on time-to-fix but on how much they are stumbling over that issue. (That is, prioritize by impact, not cost.) This is usually better than prioritizing by time-to-fix, but still risks tackling some daunting tasks without building up that skillset.
- Highest-ROI first -- **This is what we should do.** Get estimates on both time-to-fix as well as impact. **Both are going to be estimates.** As hard as it may be to estimate the time requirement to implement a new feature, cleanup time depends on so many more unknown unknowns and it is common for time estimates to be off by 5x or more. Estimating the "value," in terms of productivity gain, is similarly going to be imprecise. In both cases, simple rough-estimate approaches are appropriate. Planning Poker and other rough-consensus estimation tools may be appropriate.
  
  Armed with a prioritization, hopefully the Highest-ROI prioritization, we can begin to schedule and plan where needed. With even a rough summary of these dimensions, a matrix like this can help us prioritize, make progress, and maintain credibility for the program.
  
  | **Time-to-fix / Impact** | **Low impact** | **Medium Impact** | **High Impact** |
  | ---- | ---- | ---- |
  | **Months/quarters** | Won't fix, backlog. | Potential Fixit Week project, or low-priority annual planning item. Otherwise, Backlog. | High-priority OKR / Annual planning item |
  | **Days/weeks** | Won't fix, backlog. | Medium-priority epic / sprint planning item | High-priority epic / sprint planning item |
  | **Minutes/Hours** | Campsite Rule | Just Do It | Just Do It |
  
  Let's look at these 7 categories in more detail:
- **Low-impact, days/weeks/months/quarters to fix**: These are your deep backlog items. In many cases, these will live in your TD inventory forever - they are not sufficiently valuable to fix, or they would take forever, or both.\ \ Importantly, **leave these in your inventory**. Over time, things may change. It may be that you get better tools, tactics, and skill at resolving TD, and the time-to-fix estimate may drop enough to put this in scope. Or the seriousness of not handling this may increase (this is particularly common with deferred maintenance and upgrades). Leaving these in the inventory ensures that we have some shared awareness that it may need to happen, and provides a clear source-of-truth for engineers to contribute any notes about the issue if they do any additional investigation.\ \ Just as importantly, having a clear backlog of tasks judged to have insufficient ROI sends a good signal to higher-level leaders: you know that this is an engineering matter, and are prioritizing based on good bang-for-the-buck. Showing that the team recognizes some issues to be out of scope when considering task planning demonstrates that the team isn't taking your TD mandate as permission to fix **everything**.
- **Medium/high impact, minutes/hours to fix**: This is the "Just do it" zone. Having any significant discussion about whether or when to fix these is unnecessary and inefficient. Go ahead and do these, ideally from highest-impact to lowest.\ \ Teams may choose to tackle these by having a burndown list ("tackle one of these when you've got an hour"), an organized effort ("we will all spend one day/week and do as many of these as possible"), or by giving people general permission to tidy them up as they come across them. The important point is agreeing that they are valuable, and not wasting any human time debating *whether* to tackle them or *how* valuable they are. With such small items, the smallest bit of friction may dilute the ROI past the point we're justified in fixing the issue.
- **Low-impact, minutes/hours to fix**: This is the area that has the most discretion for the team. These issues probably won't add up to much pain, but also won't take much time to fix. You might discuss and decide that you're going to treat it as an extension of the Backlog zone. Or you may decide to treat it as an extension of the "Just Do It" zone. The balanced approach is probably to exclude it from any burndown lists or concerted efforts, but to encourage fixes to these items as part of team [Campsite Rule](https://wolfgang-ziegler.com/blog/Development-Practice-The-Campsite-Rule.aspx) or [Prefactoring](https://www.jvt.me/posts/2022/04/12/prefactor/). These approaches suggest "This is worth cleaning up if you're already doing work in the area, but it's probably not worth doing on its own."
- **High-impact, months/quarters to fix**: These are your headlining, capital-investment projects. You may want to put off investing in these long-term plans until you've gotten comfortable running some shorter-term high-impact projects and confirmed that you've got long-term support for this work.\ \ In the steady state, one or more projects of this size/scope should be present in any quarterly/annual planning process.
- **Medium-impact, months/quarters to fix:** These probably get deferred until the high-impact long-term projects are tackled. However, in some cases (especially if a [Swarming](https://www.techtarget.com/searchsoftwarequality/opinion/Agile-pairing-and-Agile-swarming-jumpstart-projects) strategy could apply), the team may decide to go all-in on such a task for a short period of time. This may be a good use of a recovery week after meeting a tough milestone release, or a dedicated "fixit" week, especially if it's the type of TD item that weighs heavily on developer morale even without having a huge provable impact.
- **High-impact, days/weeks to fix:** These are the best tasks to *start* a TD program with - high value, requiring planning and coordination, but without taking so long that people become frustrated or disheartened. These tend to sound a little smaller than the longer-term projects, and may not be worth reporting about to higher-level managers.\ \ Since these are short-term, these are good filler for projects with complex dependencies that might leave "bubbles" where a developer or sub-team is blocked on other work.
- **Medium-impact, days/weeks to fix:** If anything in your TD inventory is worth tracking over time, it's probably the number of issues in this bucket. If this only grows and we never manage to get around to any of these, either our "Won't fix/backlog" is too small, or we aren't systemically devoting enough energy to TD projects to hold the line.
  
  A few other notes about prioritization and task assignment:
- **Try to assign items (especially multi-month tasks) to pairs, not individuals**: Because TD cleanup is a novel skill, it is extra valuable to have someone to bounce ideas off of, to pair program with, to help plan, etc. Just as critically, as we are first building up those skills, our initial estimates of task duration are probably terrible. Having a pair of developer perspectives to explain everything that went wrong and all the details makes it much easier to understand and believe them. This (hopefully) mitigates some of the initial perception of low-ROI for TD work, and makes it easier to continue support even when the first few tasks seem to take surprisingly long.
- **Re-evaluate your prioritization periodically**: As we up-skill and gain better tools for handling our tech debt issues, our estimates of what items are hard or easy may shift. It is worth re-evaluating even the "Won't Fix" backlog items periodically (quarterly or annually).
- **Remember that everything is an estimate, don't worry too much about specificity**: If Alice is better than Bob at doing compiler upgrades, but fixing a race condition in the release testing infrastructure is listed slightly higher than updating the compiler, it may still be reasonable to let Alice do the compiler upgrade and wait for Bob to have some availability before tackling the higher-priority test issue.
- ### Balancing Between Features and Productivity Improvements [#](https://devhome.corp.adobe.com/docs/default/component/whitepapers/tech-debt-management/#balancing-between-features-and-productivity-improvements)
  
  One of the most common hurdles for teams that want to address their TD issues is the feeling that their leadership doesn't see the value or want to prioritize that work. New features and measures of revenue are prioritized above all else.
  
  In practice, that is true some of the time, but not as often as we assume. We often face a slightly different problem: **Leadership is supportive, but not close enough to the problem to say what to do.** This is very understandable: many engineering leaders are conceptually supportive of this type of work, but because they are removed from the day-to-day grind of task assignment, design, code, build, test, and release, they aren't familiar enough with the specific to suggest (or approve) of specific work that needs to be done.
  
  Simultaneously, many new front-line leaders don't commonly see infrastructure and productivity and TD items explicitly added to roadmaps, and thus don't feel empowered to suggest those. Without higher-level leadership making suggestions or asking questions about productivity and TD (along with other important-not-urgent issues), it is easy for it to become ignored.
  
  To address this, leadership (at all levels) should regularly be asking questions about productivity, hurdles, and what the team is doing that is "important but not urgent." Make sure there is enough slack in the schedule for individuals and full teams to find time to address some of these systemic issues. Leaders should set goals around measurable and mechanical indicators of quality and productivity: amount of human time involved in cutting a release, average time between releases, etc. Those types of goals give product teams permission to at least address TD issues around testing and release automation, which is a great place to start with TD management.
  
  A team that is tasked 100% with *anything* is likely to burn out, produce messy work, and slow down in the long term. I strongly recommend a "fuel mixture" closer to 70/30 or 80/20 - most effort for the team is devoted (sensibly) toward new features and generating revenue. But 20% to 30% of the time is held in reserve. That isn't permission to take off an extra day every week, or blow several hours a day playing ping-pong. Rather, we use that reserve time for **important-not-urgent** work: TD reduction, education, team bonding, and all of the things that make us better at doing our job. In cases where we have a challenging short-term deadline (a month or so, tops) we can borrow against that reserve without heroics or burning people out. And in the normal case, we have the chance to build up our skills and pay down our debts.
- ### An Alternate View on Costs and Estimates
  
  The "fuel mixture" viewpoint above likely works best for leaders with an engineering background and a focus on medium- or long-term results. Not all leaders want to know those details, or to be involved in approving that balance. In those cases, teams may want to make space for their essential TD work in a different fashion: including their overhead in their estimates.
  
  As anyone with experience in contract bidding or academic grant writing can attest, estimates for a project in other contexts include more than the raw development cost. An academic grant will include labor and resource costs to do the research, additional funding for travel, and then the grant department adds a substantial amount (often around 50%) to account for the overhead of the institution: utilities, janitorial staff, office staff, etc. Similarly, in contract bidding engineering domains, the base work is a part of the bid, but the total is inflated to account for overhead and profit.
  
  If product owners primarily do planning and prioritization via estimates around new feature work, and never allow for slack in the system for important-not-urgent activities, it may be necessary to carve out that space for your team by accounting for your overhead in those estimates. Any contract- or grant-based organization that fails to account for the cost of its overhead is doomed to fail. The failure modes for our teams are less dire, as our teams do not go bankrupt, but the same model may apply: if you're not given support from above, and you are falling behind on maintenance and TD issues, you may have to add (reasonable) buffer to your work estimates in order to ensure you can metaphorically keep the lights on and pay the janitors.
- ### An Escalation Path 
  
  If we return to the "technical pollution" model, what happens when the team starts running into a problem that is too big for them to tackle? Or that requires coordination across the broader product or organization? Unlike the finance metaphor, a pollution mindset gives us a lot of guidance for what to do when our hurdles become so daunting and widespread that they cannot be handled internally: escalate to experts.
  
  For small, isolated teams, hopefully this doesn't arise. For teams sharing a large codebase, tragedy of the commons issues may arise that require exactly this handling. It is important to have a good working relationship with partner teams. One useful point of synchronization and shared planning with those teams is to track systemic TD, agree on priorities among those larger issues, and find mechanisms to mitigate or address those. It may become necessary to spin up platform-wide infrastructure teams that handle such things, or short-term tiger teams for specific issues. In essence, these approaches are a recognition that some TD issues will require long-term specialist work. These may require special funding and discussion with management.
  
  Like other forms of TD (or Superfund sites), it is unlikely that these bigger issues will be universally resolved, much less resolved quickly. As with team-level TD management, the goal is to track it, prioritize it, and ensure that the issues are not getting unboundedly worse. While these likely require higher-level visibility and support, the nature of the problems being tackled tend to be easier to sell to management: upgrading language versions, supporting new OS versions, complying with industry practices, security requirements, or governmental mandates.
  
  If there are TD items lingering in your inventory that are simply too out-of-scope and too daunting for your team to imagine handling, escalate them. If you don't have an escalation path yet, that is your next task!
- ## A Tech Debt Maturity Model 
  
  Some teams like to set goals about the scale of their *process* improvements. For such teams, it may be appropriate to set goals around integrating more mature tech debt management practices into their working process. A maturity model for Tech Debt might look like this:
- **Reactive** - We only address tech debt issues individually, and only when they become a crisis. This likely limits such work to last-minute upgrades away from end-of-life systems, or reactions to security vulnerability notifications. There is likely no inventory, and the procedure to get non-feature work into the schedule is poorly understood, if it is codified at all.
- **Tactical** - The most pressing TD issues are tracked somewhere (not necessarily alongside feature requests). Very few issues (if any) become urgent / high-priority / deadline-driven work.
- **Strategic** - There is a robust inventory of TD issues, the team regularly contributes new items to the inventory, and the process of prioritizing items from that inventory is well understood. No issues are deferred long enough to be an urgent / high-priority deadline.
- **Mature** - There is a robust inventory of TD issues, Product Management and TPMs understand the balance between new features and TD work. Task assignment naturally includes a mix of features and productivity work, with a shifting balance as product priorities shift (but never dipping below 15% planning commitment for TD). Developer sentiment for "Having enough time to balance technical debt" is above 75%, and estimated time loss due to inefficiencies is at most 20% (both of those are reported in the periodic Adobe Developer Survey).
  
  Even without formally adopting these definitions or levels, setting goals to up-level the team's performance in these areas is a useful strategy. While most TD issues individually don't rise to the level of importance to merit attention from higher-level leaders, setting goals around process improvement and capability building may be a good way to highlight the work without getting lost in the details.
- ## Final Tips
- If you still have them, empower your QEs. Quality Engineers are the natural front line of defense against TD issues. If we use them purely as guardians and reactions against technical issues, we cannot really achieve product quality. If we empower them to push back on messy code and questionable designs, and to advocate for redesign work and TD efforts, it is easier to imagine long-term success.
- If you don't have QEs, it's useful for someone on each front-line team to be specifically tasked with being the advocate for quality, and for devoting the necessary time to TD management. Without having *someone* that is both empowered and incentivized to hold the line on quality, it is easy for everyone to let things slide.
- Don't go alone. When starting TD management programs, find other teams that are also starting work in the area, and work with them to refine your learning and understanding of the problem in your domain. Even if your buddy team is just starting out, it is invaluable to have a forum where the TD leaders can summarize what they've done, what is working, what isn't, where they have questions and concerns.
- Estimation and planning for TD work is very hard. Undoing a mess is harder than making a mess, and very often you can't be sure that the full scope of the issue is understood until you are very nearly done cleaning it up. There can always be something scary lurking in that last dark corner.
- Cleanup work is a skill. Individual engineers and whole teams will get better with practice. Don't get discouraged!
- Don't tackle the hardest things first, even if they seem to have the best ROI. Get a few easy wins in place first, build some momentum and excitement.
- Share your stories! There should be just as much visibility, celebration, excitement, and recognition (including bonuses) for landing a long-running TD cleanup effort as for a long-running feature development epic. The more that we normalize talking about the wins and the challenges, the more it stops feeling like cleanup work is abnormal.

- **The Satir Process of Change Model describes five stages—Late Status Quo, Resistance/Foreign Element, Chaos, Integration, and New Status Quo—showing how individuals and groups emotionally and behaviorally move through change, as developed by Virginia Satir in the 1960s.**
- ## Overview
  
  The Satir Process of Change Model (often called the Satir Change Model) explains the human emotional journey during change. It originated from Virginia Satir’s work in family therapy and was later adapted for organizational change. The model highlights that change is not only structural but deeply psychological
- ## The Five Stages
- ### 1.  **Late Status Quo**
- A stable, familiar, predictable state.
- People feel comfortable but may also experience stagnation or dissatisfaction.
- Routines dominate, and change seems unnecessary or risky.

- ### 2.  **Resistance / Foreign Element**
- A new factor disrupts the status quo (e.g., new policy, technology, market shift).
- People may deny, ignore, or resist the change.
- Emotions include fear, anxiety, defensiveness, and confusion.

- ### 3.  **Chaos**
- Old patterns no longer work; confusion and instability rise.
- Productivity often drops (“J‑curve” dip).
- People may feel overwhelmed, disoriented, or uncertain.

- ### 4.  **Integration / Transforming Idea**
- A new idea or approach begins to make sense.
- Individuals start experimenting with new behaviors and learning new skills.
- Confidence gradually returns as people adapt.

- ### 5.  **New Status Quo**
- The change becomes integrated and normalized.
- Performance stabilizes at a higher level than before.
- People feel more competent, aligned, and comfortable with the new system.

- ## Why the Model Matters
- Helps leaders anticipate emotional reactions to change.
- Normalizes resistance and confusion as part of the process.
- Supports planning interventions (training, coaching, communication) at the right stage.

- ## Summary
  
  The Satir Process of Change Model provides a human-centered framework showing how people move from stability, through disruption and chaos, toward a more effective new equilibrium.

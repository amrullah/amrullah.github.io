- # Key Ideas of this Chapter
    - The information in [[WM]] is like a set of balls being juggled by an octopus. Too many balls at once and the octopus can be overwhelmed.
    - The [[neural]] links of [[LTM]] can activate and extend [[WM]]
    - Within any one classroom, there is a great deal of variability in [[WM]] capacity among the students.
    - Differences in [[WM]] capacity can. mean differences in learning speed. This in turn can demand different teaching approaches so that each student can be successful - and neither overwhelmed nor underwhelmed.
    - Teaching strategies geared for those with lesser-capacity [[WM]] can often be useful for all students.
    - Breaking information and activities into smaller conceptual parts will combat too-heavy demands on students' [[WM]].
    - Students with lesser-capacity [[WM]] and all students learning new, unfamiliar information, benefit from teacher-directed approaches. As students gain fluency, instruction can move toward more independent, student-directed approaches.
    - Break up your presentation of new information with active exercises to assist with [[consolidation]] - when brain strengthens it's new neural links.
- # Introduction
    - Most learners are race cars on some topics and hikers on others.
        - In short, they quickly grasp [[Concepts]] in some topics and struggle to wrap their head around other topics.
        - But speed is not necessarily an advantage.
            - The race-car driver gets to the finish line quickly but everything goes by in a blur.
            - Whereas the hiker can absorb the scenery on the path much better and in a richer and deeper way.
        - There are two different [[neural]] pathways to [[Learning]], which may relate to race-car and hiker approaches to [[Learning]].
            - [[Declarative neural pathway]]
            - [[Procedural neural pathway]]
        - One prominent advantage of slow [[Learning]] mentioned by the [[Authors]] is the flexibility, way of thinking and the ability to change one's mind, when wrong. Something the "geniuses" may miss out on due to having little practice in acknowledging and correcting errors

    - The *Central Executive* keeps a piece of information in mind by tossing it and reverberating it between the Focus of Attention, seeing and hearing networks and itself.
        - This roundabout movement keeps the thoughts alive and is also why one can only hold so much information in their minds at once.
    - [[Working Memory]] [[Neurons]] are different from those involved in [[Long Term Memory]]. They, unlike [[Long Term Memory]]'s [[Neurons]], can't hold the information for very long.
        - Fortunately, if you've [[learned]] information and stored it in [[Long Term Memory]], that information can link into and boost your [[Working Memory]].
    - Experienced teachers know that depending on the complexity of the task, they may need to give one instruction, wait for students to complete it, then give the next instruction. Or they might write the instructions on a whiteboard, so even if the information falls out of students' [[Working Memory]], the list is there anyway.
- # How Differences in [[Working Memory]] impact your classroom
    - Students' varying [[Working Memory]] capacities have an impact on you as a teacher in the classroom.
    - Young children's [[Working Memory]] capacity is quite low. It increases as the child matures. By the time they are fourteen, they have an average adult sized [[Working Memory]], which is twice what they had when they were four year old.

        - ![Working Memory growth curve](..assets/uncommon_sense_learning_illustrations/Fig_8_pg_20_working_memory_capcity_with_age.png)

    - Students with [[Working Memory]] deficits often struggle as tasks get more demanding. They also lose track when it comes to more complex activities

    - ## Strategies to help students with lesser capacity [[Working Memory]]
        - Be brief and as linguistically simple as possible with your directions.
        - Make sure your students are looking at you when you are giving directions
        - Give instructions one at a time, including "step checks" to make sure everyone is with you.
        - Provide instructions on the board or as a checklist on students' desks
        - Use mnemonics
- # Strengthen [[Working Memory]] by building [[Long Term Memory]]
    - Those with lesser capacity [[Working Memory]] can struggle more with their [[Learning]], but [[Long Term Memory]] can ultimately become a part of [[Working Memory]], especially with [[Retrieval Practice]].
        - The more assistance [[Working Memory]] gets from the prior knowledge stored in [[Long Term Memory]], the easier it is for students, especially those with less capacity in their [[Working Memory]], to [[learn]] new material.
        - The more knowledge held in the [[Long Term Memory]], easier it is to add more.
    - There are several ways to get information into [[Long Term Memory]]
        - One way, the [[Declarative Learning System]] uses [[Working Memory]]
        - Another fascinating way, the [[Procedural Learning System]] will be explored ahead.
